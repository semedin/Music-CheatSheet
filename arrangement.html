Field guide 06 · arrangement · transitions · energy

A track is
a shape
over time

Loops are easy. What separates a loop from a record is that the energy goes somewhere — up, down, and up again — across six or seven minutes, in blocks of eight, sixteen and thirty-two bars. This page lets you build that shape, hear any point in it instantly, and export the whole map as a multi-track MIDI file you can drag straight into Live as a scaffold.

Section 02 · eight structures

Templates

Real arrangement shapes with real bar counts. The differences between them are mostly about who the track is for — a DJ needs thirty-two bars of beats to mix into, a streaming listener needs the hook inside twenty seconds, and those two demands are irreconcilable, which is why club edits and radio edits exist separately.

Section 03 · interactive

Transitions lab

A four-bar loop is running. Every pad below fires in time — press it and the effect is armed for the next bar line, the way it would be if you were performing it. That timing is the point: transitions that land off the grid sound like mistakes, and this is the fastest way to feel where each one belongs.

Fire a transition

idle

The one people get wrong is silence. A single beat of nothing before a drop does more than any riser, costs nothing, and needs no plugin — but it has to be exactly one beat, exactly on the bar line, and it only works once or twice in a track. Try firing "Cut to silence" and then "Riser" and notice which one you actually believe.

Section 04

Transition toolkit

What each device is for, how long it should run, and where it goes.

DeviceLengthWhereHow to build it
Low-pass sweep down8–16 barsInto a breakdownAuto Filter on the drum bus, cutoff automated 20 kHz → 400 Hz. Add resonance as it closes.
High-pass build8–16 barsBefore a dropHigh-pass everything except the kick, sweeping up. Removes the bottom so the drop's return of bass is the event.
Noise riser4–8 barsBefore a dropWhite noise, band-pass sweeping up, amp fading in. Should be inaudible when it starts.
Pitch riser2–4 barsLast bars of a buildA saw with a pitch envelope climbing an octave. More aggressive than noise; use once.
Snare / clap roll1–2 barsFinal bars of a build8ths → 16ths → 32nds with rising velocity. The oldest build in dance music.
Impact / boom1 hitOn the downbeat of a dropLow sine drop plus a reversed cymbal. Marks the landing so the ear knows the section changed.
Reverse cymbal1–2 barsLeading into any sectionReversed crash or reversed reverb tail, ending exactly on the downbeat.
Cut to silence1 beatImmediately before a dropMute everything for one beat. The most effective and most overused device here.
Delay throw1–2 barsLast hit before a breakSend one chord or vocal to a dotted-8th delay with high feedback, then mute the source.
Reverb wash2–4 barsInto a breakdownFreeze or 100% wet a long reverb on the last chord, then pull the dry.
Tape stop1 beatSection change, rarelyPitch and speed falling to zero. Extremely recognisable, so use once per track at most.
Drum dropout1–2 barsAnywhereRemove the kick for a bar and let the top end run. Costs nothing and resets the ear.

Layer three, not one

  • A convincing drop is almost always three devices at once: something ending (a riser peaking), something absent (a beat of silence), and something arriving (an impact plus the full arrangement).
  • Everything should resolve exactly on the downbeat. A riser that peaks a 16th late reads as sloppiness, not tension.
  • The riser goes up, the filter goes down, and they can happen simultaneously — opposing motion is why big builds feel like they are tearing.
  • Transitions are the loudest moments in a track. Check the meters: a riser plus an impact plus a full arrangement is where your master clips.

Subtraction beats addition

  • To make a drop bigger, make the eight bars before it smaller. Energy is relative and nothing else.
  • Removing the kick for two bars before a drop is more effective than adding a riser, and it costs no extra sound.
  • If everything is playing by bar 33, you have nothing left to add for the next five minutes.
  • The best-arranged techno records have three or four elements playing at any moment, swapping which ones, not stacking.
Section 05

Phrasing

Dance music counts in powers of two. Breaking that is a decision, not an accident, and almost everything should sit on it.

LengthWhat it is
1 barThe loop. Almost nothing changes here.
4 barsThe phrase. One small variation — a hat pattern, a filter move.
8 barsThe sentence. Something enters or leaves. The minimum unit of arrangement.
16 barsThe paragraph. A real change of state. Most sections are a multiple of this.
32 barsThe section. Intros, main sections and outros in club tracks are usually 32 or 64.
64 barsRoughly two minutes at 126. A whole DJ mix window.

The counting rules

  • Change something every 8 bars, minimum. Not necessarily a big something. A hat that opens, a filter that lifts, one extra percussion hit.
  • Change something significant every 16. An element in or out.
  • The DJ intro is 32 bars of beats with almost no melodic content, so it can sit under the outgoing track without clashing in key. The outro mirrors it.
  • The first drop should not be the biggest. Leave headroom — the second main section is where the track should peak.
  • Breaking phrasing deliberately — a 12-bar build, a 6-bar breakdown — is a real technique in hypnotic techno, but the listener has to be able to tell it was on purpose.
Section 06

Working rules

Getting out of loop hell

  • Duplicate your loop to the full length first. Eight minutes of the same four bars, then delete downwards. Arranging by subtraction is far faster than arranging by addition.
  • Print the shape before you make sounds: intro, build, main, break, build, main, outro, with bar numbers. Two minutes of work that saves a week.
  • Give yourself a deadline per section. Loop hell is a decision problem, not a creativity problem.
  • If a section bores you at half volume, it will bore a room at full volume.

Diagnosis

  • "It doesn't go anywhere" — everything enters in the first 32 bars. Delete half of them and let elements arrive later.
  • "The drop isn't big" — the build was already at full energy. Cut the eight bars before it.
  • "It drags in the middle" — a 64-bar main section with no variation. Something has to change every eight.
  • "It sounds like a loop with edits" — the elements change but the energy never does. Watch the curve above: if it is flat, so is the track.
  • "DJs won't play it" — no clean beats-only intro, or the intro has a melody that will clash with whatever is mixing out.

In Live

  • Locators (Create → Add Locator, or Set on the master track) at every section boundary. Then the arrow keys jump you between sections.
  • Arrange in Session view first if you prefer: build scenes for each section, record the performance into Arrangement, then tidy.
  • Consolidate Time to New Scene and Duplicate Time (Ctrl+Shift+D) are the two shortcuts that make arranging fast.
  • Automate on the master or a group track, not on every channel individually. A single Auto Filter on a drum group does what twelve automation lanes do.
  • Drag the MIDI from this page in as track one and use it as a ruler while you work.

Length and audience

  • Club: 6–8 minutes, 32-bar beat intro and outro, first main section around 1:30.
  • Streaming / radio: 3–4 minutes, hook inside 20 seconds, no long intro — the skip rate in the first 30 seconds decides everything.
  • Many producers release both. The club edit is the record; the radio edit is the advert.
  • At 126 BPM, one bar is 1.9 seconds — so 32 bars is a minute. Do this arithmetic once and section lengths stop being guesswork.

The shape is the song. Everything in the other five field guides — groove, low end, drums, patches, chords — is material. This is the one that decides whether the material becomes a record, and it is the part almost everyone leaves until they are already sick of the loop. Do it second, not last.
