Field notes · 01

What each drum is for

Twelve lanes, complementary roles. Start with a rhythmic anchor, then give every added part a reason to be there.

The floor: kick

  • Four on the floor gives a steady quarter-note pulse. Kick length, accent and the parts around it determine whether that pulse feels heavy, buoyant or restrained.
  • A syncopated kick can suggest garage, breaks or electro. House also uses pickups and displaced kicks: listen to the whole phrase, not just one lane.
  • Try a sixteenth-note pickup just before the next downbeat: step 16 in bar 2 (step 32 across both bars). Lower its velocity to keep beat 1 strong.

The answer: snare & clap

  • On beats 2 and 4, they create a familiar backbeat. One hit on beat 3 instead suggests a half-time feel.
  • A clap often has several closely spaced transients; a snare can add a pitched body. Both appear across house, techno and garage.
  • Try a clap layer 10–20 ms behind a snare in your DAW. Compare against simultaneous hits; a loose flam is not always the right choice.
  • Ghost snares are quiet articulations between the main hits. Try MIDI velocity 30–50 as a starting point, then adjust for your samples.

The engine: hats

  • Hats carry the subdivision — they tell you whether the track is in 8ths, 16ths or shuffled 16ths, and that single choice does more genre work than anything except tempo.
  • The open hat on offbeat eighth notes is a familiar house device, also common in techno. Length, tone and the surrounding parts change its effect.
  • Swing lives here. Swing the hats, leave the kick straight, and the tension between them is the groove.
  • Vary hat velocity in a pattern, not randomly. Strong, weak, medium, weak.

The colour: percussion

  • Rim / clave is for syncopation. One rim on an unexpected 16th does more than eight bars of hats.
  • Shaker can add a softer rhythmic layer between the hats. Its attacks still contribute to the groove: keep it quiet enough to support the main pulse.
  • Congas and toms have resonant bodies. Think about their pitch and articulation as well as their rhythm, especially alongside a bassline.
  • Cowbell and tambourine add strong tonal or noisy accents. Both appear in many styles; sound choice and placement determine their character here.
  • Percussion should be the first thing you mute when a loop feels cluttered, and it usually turns out you don't need most of it.

Structure vs texture

  • Use the morph lab to compare these examples. Structural edits change the kick and backbeat; texture edits change the supporting detail.
  • Classic house → peak-time techno: compare the stronger hat pulse and tom turnaround, then audition the tempo and kit together.
  • Classic house → hypnotic techno: removing the clap is one possible route to a less pronounced backbeat. Many hypnotic tracks still use claps.
  • Tech house → 2-step: listen to the syncopated kick against the snare, then add the skipping hats and shuffle.
  • Check the kick and backbeat first, then the subdivision, swing and sound palette. Each can change how a groove reads.

Density discipline

  • Compare hit density at the same tempo and kit. Density changes the energy, but sustain and velocity matter just as much as the number of notes.
  • Overlapping hits can create useful layers. If the transient gets blurry, try a different sample, lower velocity or a small timing offset in your DAW.
  • The best test for a busy loop: mute everything except kick and one other part. If that doesn't groove, adding parts won't fix it.
  • Silence on the downbeat of a bar is one of the strongest arrangement tools there is, and it costs nothing.
