Section 01 · interactive

The answer

Set the key your sample is in and the key your track is in. The number is what you type into the Transpose field in Clip View. Press play and you will hear the sample against your track's chords — raw, then shifted.

ShiftResultNotes outside your keyVerdict

What you are hearing. The sample is rendered fresh into an audio buffer in whatever key you select, so it genuinely is in that key. Shifting it changes the playback rate exactly as Ableton's Transpose does in Re-Pitch mode — which is why large shifts sound like a chipmunk. That artifact is real, not a simulation, and it is the reason section 5 exists.

Start here if you are new to this: set the sample to C major and your track to A minor, and look at the number. It is zero. Those two keys contain exactly the same seven notes — C major is the relative major of A minor — so a sample labelled C major drops straight into an A minor track untouched. Half the transposing people do is unnecessary.

Section 02

Lookup grid

No thinking required. Find your sample's root down the side, your project's root across the top, read the number. It is always the shortest of the two possible shifts, so it never asks you to move more than six semitones. Your current selection is outlined.

This grid assumes both keys are the same type — minor to minor, or major to major. If your sample is major and your track is minor, use the calculator above instead, because the relative-key shortcut usually gives you a better answer than raw root-to-root arithmetic. Green cells are free or nearly free; red means you are asking a lot of the algorithm.

Section 03 · interactive

The wheel

The Camelot wheel that DJ software prints on everything, with the actual key names on it. Click any segment to set your project key. The lit segments are the ones that mix with it without a fight.

MoveMeansEffect

Why adjacent keys work. Two keys one step apart on the wheel share six of their seven notes — they differ by exactly one. That is not a convention someone invented for DJs, it is arithmetic, and it is why the calculator above will often tell you a one-note clash is worth leaving alone.

Section 04 · interactive

Find the key of a sample

Splice tags are wrong often enough that it is worth checking, and plenty of material has no tag at all. No software here can analyse a file you have not given it — but the manual method takes two minutes and always works.

Step 1Loop your sample Step 2Click through the drones Step 3One will sit still Step 4Then test major vs minor

Play your sample on loop in Ableton, then click these one at a time while it plays. Eleven of them will grind against it. One will disappear into it — that is your root. Sustained notes and the last note of a phrase are the easiest places to hear it.

no drone

Then major or minor. Once you have the root, play both triads against the sample. The one that vanishes into it is your answer; the other will sound sour on its third. If neither is comfortable the sample is probably modal — try Dorian if it feels minor but bright, and Phrygian if it feels minor and dark.

Two shortcuts worth knowing. A bass line usually states the root on the downbeat, so a sample with bass in it is far easier to pitch than a vocal alone. And if the sample ends on a long note, that note is the root roughly nine times out of ten.

Section 05

What transposing costs

Every semitone you move a sample damages it slightly. How much depends entirely on what the material is.

ShiftVocalsMelodic / chordsDrums & percussion
0FreeFreeFree
±1–2Essentially inaudibleInaudibleInaudible
±3–4Character starts to move — noticeable on a lead vocal, fine on a chopFineFine, and often desirable
±5–6Obviously processed. Needs Complex Pro with Formants onUsually still fineFine — this is how you get a different kit
±7 or moreFind another sampleThin going up, sluggish going downStill works, but it is a new sound rather than the same one

Why vocals are the hard case

  • A voice has formants — fixed resonances of the singer's throat and mouth that do not move when they sing higher. Repitching moves them anyway, which is exactly what makes a pitched-up vocal sound like a cartoon.
  • Complex Pro's Formants control holds them still while the pitch moves. Turn it on before you decide a transposed vocal is unusable.
  • Down is more forgiving than up. A vocal dropped four semitones sounds thick; the same vocal raised four sounds like a child.
  • The formant button in the calculator above applies a crude version of this so you can hear the difference. It is a filter-bank approximation, not Ableton's algorithm.

Cheaper alternatives to transposing

  • Transpose your project instead. If the vocal is the hook and everything else is MIDI, move your own parts. Free and artifact-free.
  • Use the relative key. Check the calculator first — the answer is often zero.
  • Chop out the offending note. If only one note of a sample clashes, cut it rather than pitching the whole thing.
  • Pitch only some of it. A vocal chop and a sustained vocal do not need the same treatment; chops survive far more transposition than held notes.
Section 06 · interactive

Repitch changes tempo

If a clip is set to Re-Pitch, or unwarped, pitch and speed are locked together like a turntable. That means every tempo decision is secretly a key decision, and vice versa. This catches everyone at least once.

The useful direction is backwards. If you need a 94 BPM sample to sit in a 126 BPM track and you want to repitch rather than stretch, the calculator tells you that is +5.07 semitones — so a sample in F♯ minor arrives in B minor, not F♯ minor. Work that out before you write eight bars of chords in the wrong key.

Section 07

In Ableton

Where the controls actually are

  • Clip View → Transpose is the semitone field. Detune next to it is in cents, 100 to a semitone, for fixing a sample that is slightly flat.
  • Warp mode changes what Transpose does. In Re-Pitch it moves tempo with it; in every other mode the tempo stays put.
  • Complex Pro exposes Formants and Envelope. Formants on for vocals, Envelope lower for tonal material and higher for percussive.
  • Simpler has its own Transpose knob, which is the one to use if you are playing a sample chromatically from MIDI.
  • The Pitch MIDI effect does nothing to audio. It only transposes MIDI notes on the way to an instrument, and reaching for it on an audio track is the single most common beginner mistake here.

Live 12 specifically

  • Live 12 has a global Key and Scale setting, and clips can be tagged with a key so the set stays coherent. It does not analyse audio to work out what key a sample is in — that part is still section 4's job.
  • Auto Shift (12.1) is a real-time pitch-correction device that follows the global key. It is aimed at monophonic vocals rather than at transposing a whole loop, but for a vocal that is nearly right it is faster than transposing.
  • Auto Shift also does real-time formant shifting independently of pitch, which is worth knowing about even when you are not correcting anything.
  • Scale-aware MIDI plus Fold to scale means once your project key is set, writing wrong notes gets much harder.

A workflow that avoids the problem

  • Decide your key before you go sample hunting. Then filter Splice by that key and its relative — most of the work disappears.
  • Rename files with the key in them the moment you download. Future you will not remember.
  • Check the tag. Drop the sample in, play the drone from section 4, confirm. Ten seconds against an hour of confusion later.
  • Commit early. Transpose and resample to a new audio file once you are sure, so the rest of the project sees the corrected version.

Diagnosis

  • "It sounds nearly right but sour" — one note out. Check the calculator: a single foreign note is often a shift of one on the wheel.
  • "It sounds like a chipmunk" — you pitched up too far without Formants. Try down instead of up.
  • "It went out of key when I changed the project tempo" — the clip is in Re-Pitch or unwarped.
  • "The tag says F minor but it fights everything" — the tag is wrong, or it is the relative major. Run the drone test.
  • "It is in key and still sounds bad" — the key is not the problem. It is probably register or masking, which is a different guide.

Most of this collapses into one habit. Work out the key of anything you import, immediately, before it becomes part of an arrangement you have grown attached to. Everything else on this page is arithmetic, and arithmetic is easy — it is only ever hard because it gets discovered too late.

Demo audio is synthesised and rendered fresh into a buffer in whichever key you select; the transposition is genuine playback-rate repitching. Formant correction here is a filter-bank approximation rather than Ableton's algorithm.
