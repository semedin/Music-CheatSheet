Section 02

What the controls do

Not definitions — what each one is for, and the mistake people make with it.

Oscillators: pick your raw material

  • Saw has every harmonic. It is the default for anything you intend to filter, because there is something there to remove. Bass, leads, stabs, pads.
  • Square has only odd harmonics — hollow and woody. Organs, garage stabs, chiptune-adjacent leads. Pulse-width modulation lives here.
  • Triangle is a sine with a little grit. Soft sub-adjacent bass, mellow plucks.
  • Sine has one harmonic — the fundamental. Nothing to filter, so a sine's character comes entirely from pitch, envelope and distortion. Subs and FM carriers.
  • Noise has all frequencies at once. Not a note, a texture: hats, risers, breath, the click on a kick.
  • The mistake: reaching for a complex wavetable when a saw plus a filter would have done it, and then having nothing left to shape.

The filter: remove, don't add

  • Cutoff is the single most expressive control on any synth. Most of what people automate in dance music is one cutoff.
  • Resonance boosts a narrow band right at the cutoff. Below about 4 it adds character; above 10 the filter starts to ring as a tone of its own, which is the entire acid sound.
  • High resonance plus a low cutoff can produce enormous energy at one frequency. Watch your levels — this is the most common way to blow a mix up.
  • Keytracking makes the cutoff follow the note. Without it, high notes sound duller than low ones, which is usually wrong for leads and usually fine for bass.
  • The mistake: boosting with EQ what you should have left in with the filter.

Envelopes: where the character actually is

  • Attack decides whether a sound is percussive or swelling. Anything under 5 ms reads as an immediate hit; over 100 ms it becomes a pad.
  • Decay and sustain together are the shape. Fast decay to zero sustain is a pluck; no decay and full sustain is an organ. This one relationship distinguishes most instrument families.
  • Release is what happens after the note ends. Long release on a bassline is why the low end turns to mud.
  • The filter envelope is the one that matters most in dance music. A fast filter decay over a sustained amp envelope gives you a plucked attack on a held note — that is the sound of almost every house bass ever made.
  • The mistake: shaping with the amp envelope what should have been shaped with the filter envelope. The amp envelope changes loudness; the filter envelope changes timbre over time, and timbre is what your ear reads as an instrument.

Movement: the fifth decision

  • Unison and detune — several copies of the same oscillator slightly apart in pitch. Small amounts (5–12 cents) thicken; large amounts (25–50) become the supersaw. Detune eats mono compatibility, so keep it out of the sub.
  • LFO on cutoff is the wobble, the pulse, the movement in a pad. Slow rates read as evolution, fast rates as texture.
  • LFO on pitch at 4–7 Hz is vibrato; above 20 Hz it becomes FM and changes timbre instead of pitch.
  • Pitch envelope is underrated: a fast drop into the note is a kick, a tom, a zap, and the front of a hoover.
  • The mistake: adding movement to fix a boring patch when the problem was the envelope.

Section 04

Working rules

Build in this order

  • 1. Waveform. Pick the raw material with the filter wide open, so you hear what you actually have.
  • 2. Filter position. Find the cutoff by ear with the envelope off.
  • 3. Filter envelope. This is where the instrument appears.
  • 4. Amp envelope. Length and gate. Usually shorter than you think.
  • 5. One moving thing. Unison, LFO, or pitch envelope. One, not three.
  • Every recipe on this page follows exactly that order, and you can watch it happen.

Things that are not synthesis

  • A huge amount of what people credit to a patch is effects: saturation, chorus, delay, reverb, and a compressor. A plain saw through a good chain beats a complex patch through nothing.
  • Saturation is the most valuable one. It adds harmonics above the fundamental, which is how a 50 Hz sub becomes audible on a phone.
  • Note length and velocity do more for a bassline than any oscillator choice. That is the previous field guide's argument and it holds here.
  • If the patch only sounds good playing one note, it is not a patch yet — play your actual part while designing.

Where these live in Live

  • Wavetable — closest to this lab. Two oscillators with wavetable positions, sub, noise, two filters, three envelopes, two LFOs. Start here for basses and leads.
  • Operator — four operators, FM. Use it when you want metallic, bell-like or growling tones that subtraction cannot reach.
  • Analog — two oscillators, two filters, simplest routing. Fastest for classic subtractive sounds and it sounds good doing nothing clever.
  • Drift — one oscillator plus a sub, deliberately limited. Excellent for basses precisely because there is nothing to fiddle with.
  • Whichever you pick, learn one properly rather than three badly. The controls are the same controls.

Diagnosis

  • "Thin" — no sub oscillator, or the detune is cancelling the low end. Add a single-voice sine an octave down.
  • "Muddy" — too much sustain, too long a release, or two oscillators at the same octave. Shorten before you EQ.
  • "Boring" — the filter envelope is doing nothing. Set the cutoff low, envelope amount high, decay short, and listen again.
  • "Harsh" — resonance too high with the cutoff in the 2–5 kHz range, which is exactly where ears are most sensitive.
  • "Disappears in the mix" — all the energy is below 200 Hz. Saturate, or add a quiet second oscillator an octave up.

The reason to build rather than browse. A preset teaches you nothing, because you arrive at the end without having seen any of the decisions. Every one of these ten patches is five or six choices made in a fixed order, and once you have watched that order happen a few times you stop needing presets — you hear a sound and know roughly which five decisions produced it.

Everything synthesised live in the browser. The engine is a real subtractive synth with a single filter and simplified FM — it demonstrates the relationships accurately and will not replace Wavetable.
