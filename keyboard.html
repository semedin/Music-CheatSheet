Section 01 · interactive

The keyboard

Twelve notes, then it repeats. That is the whole instrument. The black keys are grouped in twos and threes so you can find your place without counting: C is the white key just left of any pair of black keys. F is the white key just left of any group of three. Everything else is one white key along from one of those.

Two octaves, exactly what your controller has

Click a key, or play it on your controller once it is connected. The readout names what is held and, if you hold more than one note, names the chord. Turn NAMES off in the bar once you can find C without them — that is the first thing to learn and the only thing to memorise.

Computer keyboard works too. The same map Ableton uses: A is C, W is C♯, S is D, and so on along the home row to K for the next C, then L and ;. Z and X shift octaves. Not a substitute for the controller, but enough to work through every lab on this page on a laptop.

Three facts, no more

  • Twelve notes repeat. C D E F G A B are the white keys; the five black keys are the sharps and flats between them. The thirteenth key up from any key is the same note, an octave higher — same name, same job, twice the frequency.
  • There is no black key between E–F or B–C. That gap is why the black keys cluster in twos and threes, and why you can always find C.
  • One key up or down is a semitone. Every chord and scale on this page is a count of semitones. You never need to know why — the count is the whole rule.

What the numbers on your controller mean

  • Your leftmost key is C by default — MIDI note 48, which Ableton calls C2 (Live numbers the middle C, note 60, as C3; some other apps call the same key C4). The rightmost is C4, note 72. Two octaves, fifteen white keys. This page uses Ableton's names throughout.
  • Octave ▼/▲ moves the whole thing by twelve. This is not a limitation to work around; it is the technique. Bass lives one octave down, chords live where you are, and you record them as separate takes.
  • The keyboard above follows the same range. Press the octave buttons here to see the range shift and the names change.

White keys only

The seven white keys are C major. They are also A minor — same seven notes, you just treat A as home instead of C. This is the entire reason to start in A minor: on white keys there is no wrong note. Play anything, in any order, over the bed below, and it will be in key. Everything you learn on this page transfers to other keys later by lighting up different keys; the shapes do not change.

Which keys are in the key?

Change the key in the bar above. The lit keys are the seven notes of that key; the count tells you how many black keys you have just signed up for. A minor and C major are zero. That is why they come first.

Relative keys. Every minor key shares its seven notes with a major key three semitones up. A minor and C major, E minor and G major. So each set of lit keys is really two keys; which one you are "in" is decided by which note your bass keeps returning to, not by the notes you use.

The no-wrong-notes bed

A kick, a hat, a bass on the root and a held pad. Press play and noodle on the white keys — on screen, on the computer keyboard, or on your controller. The counter records how many notes you played and how many landed inside the key. In A minor the second number is trivially 100% as long as you stay on white keys; that is the point. Switch the key to something with black keys in it and watch it fall.

Chord shapes

A chord is a hand shape, not a theory fact. Play a key, skip one, play, skip one, play — three fingers, two gaps — on white keys, and you have a chord. Slide that same shape to any other white key and you have another chord in the same key. Some come out major, some minor, one comes out diminished; the shape decided that, not you.

The shape, on every note of the key

Click a chord card or click a lit key on the keyboard to make it the root. The keyboard shows the three notes; the readout gives the name and the semitone counts that make it what it is. Add the seventh, change the inversion, and watch the span — the number of keys under your hand — change with it.

Size
Inversion

Why some come out minor. "Skip a key" skips either one or two semitones depending on whether there is a black key in the gap. Root to middle note of four semitones is a major chord; three is minor. Same shape, different gaps — the key decides the gaps for you. The seventh chord in a minor key (the B in A minor) gets two small gaps and comes out diminished. It is the one that sounds wrong on its own and right in passing.

Inversions, in hand terms

  • Root position — the shape as built. Name note at the bottom.
  • First inversion — take the bottom note, move it to the top (up an octave). Now the shape is play, skip, play, skip-two, play.
  • Second inversion — do it again. The original root is now in the middle.
  • Same three notes, same chord name, different bottom note. On a small keyboard inversions are how you keep a whole progression under one hand without jumping — the Progressions lab below measures exactly how much jumping they save.

Sevenths, in hand terms

  • One more skip: play, skip, play, skip, play, skip, play. Four fingers.
  • In A minor this gives Am7, Bm7♭5, Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7. You do not need those names yet. You need to know it is the same shape with one more note, and that it sounds like house.
  • If four notes is too many, drop the middle one — the fifth. Root, third, seventh is the cheapest good chord there is, and it fits in one hand with room to spare.

Progressions you can play

Thirty loops, from four bars of white keys to eight bars with borrowed chords and moving bass lines. Pick one, press play, and the keyboard shows each chord as it lands — copy it with your hand. Read the number, then trust your hand. "Total travel" is how many semitones all the voices move across the whole loop, added up; "per change" divides it by the number of chord changes so a four-bar and an eight-bar loop can be compared. Root position on the classic loop is roughly seven times the travel of auto voicing with the same four chords. The auto version is what a pianist would do without thinking; the root version is what a piano roll does by default. That gap is most of what "sounds like a demo" means.

How to read the families

  • Classic — all white keys, all four bars, the loops half of house is built on. Learn these first and the rest are variations.
  • Dark — mostly one borrowed chord: the major V (E major in A minor, one black key) that pulls harder than the white-key E minor, or the ♭II (B♭) that slides in from a semitone above. Techno, dark house, anything with a hood up.
  • Bright — spends its time on the major chords of the same seven notes, or on sevenths. Disco, nu-disco, French house.
  • Long — six and eight bars. The second half changes one chord and that change is the whole reason to go long: the loop gets to "come home" instead of just repeating. Six-bar loops are deliberately awkward against 8-bar phrasing, and that awkwardness is a sound.
  • Inversions & bass — the chord stays and the bass moves, or the bass stays and the chords move over it. Set voicing to as written for these; root and auto will voice them differently and you will miss the point.
  • Rhythm — two chords per bar. Same shapes, half the time on each. Garage and UK bass.

The two borrowed chords worth knowing

  • V major — E in A minor. Raise the middle note of the white-key E minor (G) one key to G♯. Every "dark" loop below uses it. It is the chord that makes the return to Am feel like a decision rather than a coincidence.
  • ♭II — B♭ in A minor. The major chord one key above home. Phrygian, Spanish, and the sound of about half of hypnotic techno. Two white keys and one black.
  • Both are one black key. If a loop's card says outside the key: 1, it is one of these.
  • Slash chords like Am/G are not new chords — same right-hand shape, left hand plays the note after the slash instead of the root. That is the entire trick behind every walking bass line in the set.

Two hands, twenty-five keys

Two octaves is enough for two hands if you are disciplined about who gets which octave. Left hand takes the bottom octave and plays one note — the root — or the root and fifth. Right hand takes the top octave and plays the chord. That is a complete house part. Anything fancier gets recorded as a second pass with the octave button moved.

Does it fit?

Pick a chord and inversion; the lab places a left-hand root in the low octave and the chord in the high octave and reports whether both fit inside the two-octave window at once. When they don't, it tells you the inversion that does — or that it is a two-pass job.

The two-pass method

  1. Pass one, chords. Octave button at default. Right hand only, or both hands if it fits. Record eight bars, loop it.
  2. Pass two, bass. New MIDI track. Octave button down one or two. Left hand only, one note per chord — the root, on beat one, or on the offbeats if it is house. You are listening to pass one while you do this, so timing takes care of itself.
  3. Pass three, if any, melody. Octave up one. One finger. Stay on white keys.
  4. Three passes, one finger to four fingers each, and the controller never ran out of keys.

Left hand: the whole job description

  • Root on beat one and hold it. Progressions section, bass on, is what this sounds like.
  • Root on the offbeats (the "and" of each beat) with the kick on the beats. This is most house bass, and you can play it with one finger.
  • Root and fifth — the shape with one skip that skips two keys (play, skip three, play). Adds weight. Costs nothing.
  • Do not play the third in the left hand below C2. Low thirds are the mud that Melody & Harmony calls the low-interval limit.

Freestyle

Improvising over a loop is three rules. Not thirty. The lab below plays a full bed — drums, bass, and whichever progression you chose in section 4 — and scores what you play against those three rules while you play it.

1

Stay on the lit keys

In the key, no exceptions until you can hear why you want one. In A minor that means white keys. If it is lit, it is allowed.

2

Chord tones on the beat

When the kick hits, be on a note of the chord that is playing. Between kicks, be anywhere in the key. The bed lights the chord tones for you.

3

Say it twice

Play a short thing. Play it again. Then change one note. Repetition is what makes it sound intentional; the first time through is never the hook.

The scored bed

Press play. The keyboard lights the current chord's notes brighter than the rest of the key. Play. Everything is recorded and exportable as MIDI. When you stop, the four numbers are your score; the first two are the rules, the third is how tight your timing was to the nearest eighth note, and the fourth is what percentage of what you played was a repeat of something you had already played — rule three, measured.

progression: from section 4
bed stopped

The export is the take. Everything you play while the bed runs is recorded with its timing. The MIDI file has three tracks — you, the chords, the bass — so the accidental good bit is never lost. Reset score clears it.

What a good score looks like. In key above 95% is the floor — below that you are not looking at the lit keys. Chord tones on the beat between 60% and 85% is the sweet spot; 100% is a chord, not a melody, and under 50% sounds like you are avoiding the tune. Timing under 40 ms is tight. Repetition above 30% is a hook; under 10% is noodling.

Drills

Five, all short, all scored, all playable on screen or from the controller. Ten minutes a day is enough: two rounds of the first two, one round each of the rest. Best scores are kept for this session only.

A ten-minute routine

  • Minute 0–2 · Find the key. Until you never hesitate on C and F. Names off in the bar.
  • Minute 2–4 · Play the chord. In A minor until it is boring, then E minor (one black key), then D minor (one black key on the other side).
  • Minute 4–6 · Scale up and down. Any octave, one finger is fine. The point is the order of the notes, not the fingering.
  • Minute 6–8 · In time. One note on beat one. The mean offset number should fall week over week; under 30 ms is where it stops mattering.
  • Minute 8–10 · Freestyle bed, section 6, one progression, no score-watching until you stop.
Section 08 · reference

In Ableton

The controller is only useful if the DAW is ready when the idea arrives. Six settings, all of them one-time.

Setup

  • Preferences → Link, Tempo & MIDI. Your controller's input needs Track on. If it also has knobs, Remote on too. Nothing plays until this is done.
  • Arm the track (the record button on the track, not the transport) and set Monitor to Auto. Play; you should hear the instrument.
  • Buffer size under Audio: 128 samples or less while recording. Latency above ~10 ms makes chords feel late and you will start playing early to compensate, which then records early.
  • Computer MIDI keyboard — the toggle top right, or M. Same map as this page's A W S E D F row.

The three safety nets

  • Scale mode. Live 12 has a scale switch next to the tempo. Set A minor there and every new clip knows its key; the piano roll highlights the scale, and folding to scale hides the rest. On the controller, add the Scale MIDI effect to the track — set it to A minor — and wrong notes are corrected on the way in. Turn it off once you don't need it, because it also hides your mistakes from you.
  • Capture MIDI — the button next to record, or Shift+Cmd/Ctrl+C. Live is always listening. Play something good without pressing record, press Capture, it appears as a clip. Use this constantly; it removes the fear of the red button.
  • Quantize after, not during. Record raw, then Cmd/Ctrl+U on the clip with amount around 50–70%. Fully quantised chords sound like the piano roll drew them; half-quantised sound like a person.

Recording the two-pass part

  • Set the loop brace to 8 bars. Turn on loop and overdub (the + button by record) so each pass adds without erasing.
  • Count-in: two bars, in Preferences → Record. You need to hear the kick before you play.
  • Record chords first, bass second on a new track (octave down), and only then think about a melody. Build Order has the argument for why this order and not the other.
  • Velocity: your controller sends it. If every chord comes in at 127, Preferences → Record → Velocity curve, or fix it in the clip with the velocity lane and a random value of ±10.

What to do with the MIDI this page exports

  • Every lab has an export. Drag the file onto an empty MIDI track; multi-track files arrive as one clip per track, at the tempo and key set in the bar. The freestyle export is your actual take.
  • Play along with it before you replace it. Same chords, your timing, then delete the imported clip. The export is a scaffold, not the part.
  • For a fuller chord editor — extensions, rootless voicings, twelve keys — go to Chords. This page stops at what fits under an untrained hand.

Roman numerals are just the scale degree the shape sits on. Capitals for major, lower case for minor, ° for diminished. i–VI–III–VII in A minor means the shape on A, on F, on C, on G. Once you can read that line you can read every progression in Chords and Melody & Harmony as a set of hand positions.

You do not need to be able to play piano. You need seven shapes, three rules and a working octave button. Everything past that is either taste, which the rest of this set is for, or technique, which is worth ten minutes a day and no more until the tracks demand it.
