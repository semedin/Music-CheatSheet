Every technique on this page is applied to identical material — one loop, one key, one tempo — so the only variable is the move itself. Press play and the page alternates four bars of the original, four bars of the transformation, forever, until you can hear exactly what changed and decide whether you want it.

⚠ These work because they are rare. Every one of them is a decision that reorients a listener, and a track wearing four of them is not four times as interesting — it is incoherent. The honest rule is one per track, placed at a moment that matters: a drop, a final chorus, the bar before a breakdown. If you cannot say which bar you would put it in, you do not need it yet.

Section 02 · twelve moves

Rhythm

These change what the listener believes is happening without changing the tempo. They are the most disorienting tools available to a producer and the least used, because a sequencer does not suggest them.

Harmony

The Voicing guide covers the vocabulary you use every day. These are the exceptions — chords that do not belong to the key, reflections, substitutions and modal shifts. One of them, once, in the right bar.

Section 04

Using them

Where a move belongs

  • The final chorus or last main section. By then the listener knows the material well enough that a change registers as a decision rather than confusion.
  • The last two bars before a drop. Half-time, silence and displacement all work here because the return to normal is itself the payoff.
  • The breakdown. Harmony moves belong here more than rhythm ones — with the drums gone there is room to hear a chord that does not belong.
  • Never in the first sixteen bars. You cannot subvert an expectation you have not established.

The reversal rule

  • Most of these are more effective going back than going out. Half-time for four bars is fine; the moment it returns to full time is the event.
  • So plan the exit before the entry. A displacement that never resolves just sounds like a mistake in the render.
  • Length matters more than the move. Four bars is usually right; eight is bold; sixteen means it is now the track, not a move.
  • If you use one at the 3/4 point and undo it, you have a structure. Most producers get this by accident once and never work out what they did.

Rhythm moves specifically

  • Keep the kick as an anchor for most of them. Displacement with the kick moving too is not a move, it is a different loop.
  • Metric modulation needs a long runway. Establish the dotted-8th figure for eight bars before you let it become the pulse, or nobody follows.
  • Phasing needs patience. The interest is in the middle of the drift, so it wants sixteen bars minimum.
  • Half-time is the one you can use in almost any genre and the one most likely to become a cliché. Once.

Harmony moves specifically

  • Change one chord, not all four. A chromatic mediant landing on bar 4 of a progression is a moment; four of them is atonal.
  • Keep the bass still when the harmony gets strange. A pedal point makes almost any chord above it sound intentional.
  • Negative harmony is a generator, not a rule. Reflect a progression, keep the two chords you like, discard the rest.
  • A modal shift that changes one note is usually stronger than a substitution that changes three. Try Dorian before you try a tritone sub.

The discipline is the content. Twenty-four moves is not a menu to work through — it is a set you should mostly ignore. The producers who use these well use one of them per record, at a moment they chose in advance, and let the other twenty-three sit unused. That restraint is the actual technique; everything above is just the catalogue.
