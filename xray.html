        How it works, and where it can be wrong

        Every method below runs in this page in plain JavaScript. The analysis is tested against a synthesized demo track whose tempo, key, sections, swing and kick note are known exactly.

        Tempo and grid

        Onsets in three drum bands (low, clap, hats) at 2 ms resolution. An autocorrelation with a tempo prior picks the rough tempo; then a straight line is fitted through every strong kick's time against its beat number. The slope is the tempo, usually to within 0.01 BPM, and the scatter is the "grid holds" figure.

        Bar 1 is where chords and whole bands change most often. Octave errors (half or double time) are possible: use ½× / 2×.

        Key and chords

        A pitch-class profile from a 4096-point spectrum (bass and treble separately, whitened so noise and hats don't count), correlated against the Krumhansl–Kessler and Temperley key profiles. Chords are triads matched per bar, with the bass note as a tie-breaker. Relative major/minor mix-ups are the classic error; the alternatives are listed.

        Loudness

        ITU-R BS.1770-4 / EBU R128: K-weighting, 400 ms blocks, absolute and relative gates for integrated loudness; 3 s windows for short-term and LRA. True peak uses 4× oversampling. A mono file is measured as dual mono, the way it plays in a stereo mix.

        Sections and lanes

        Per bar: kick onsets on the beat, low end between kicks, hat activity, mids, air, loudness and chroma. A novelty curve over a bar-by-bar similarity matrix finds the changes, biased toward 4- and 8-bar phrases. Labels (intro, break, build, drop…) follow simple, visible rules, and you can rename or move any of them.

        Groove

        For each 16th step the page averages the onset strength and its timing across every bar of the section. Swing is measured the MPC way: where the second 16th of each pair lands, as a % of the 8th (50 = straight, 66 = triplet). Push/drag is the clap's median offset from beats 2 and 4.

        Limits

        • Constant tempo is assumed. Live-played or unquantised tracks show as a loose grid.
        • Low hits include bass notes that start with a punch; the clap row can pick up snares and rimshots.
        • Above ~9 kHz only level and width are measured, not onsets.
        • Lossy files: fine for everything here, but true peak can read a little high and the top octave may be missing (the page detects the cutoff).

        Questions

        Can I use MP3s?

        Yes. Your browser decodes MP3, AAC/M4A, OGG Vorbis, Opus, FLAC and WAV (AIFF only in Safari), and X-Ray reads the bitrate and sample rate from the file itself. Tempo, key, arrangement, groove, chords and loudness come out essentially the same from a 320 kbps MP3 as from the WAV. Two things change: lossy encoders often cut everything above 16–20 kHz (the page detects that cutoff and tells you to ignore the air band), and true peak can read up to about 0.5 dB higher than the master had.

        Does anything leave my computer?

        No. There's no server. The file is read with the File API and decoded by your browser. The crate stores numbers (never audio) in this browser's localStorage.

        The BPM is half or double what I expected

        Tempo octave errors happen with half-time drums and with 170+ BPM tracks. Press ½× or 2× in section 03; the grid, groove and sections all follow.

        The sections are slightly off

        Drag a section edge on the map, use the table to merge, split, rename or relabel, or fix the grid first. The skeleton MIDI, report and crate all use your edited sections.

        Why is the kick shown as a note?

        A kick's tail is a pitched sine. If it clashes with the key, the low end fights itself. X-Ray measures the tail from 90 ms after each hit, removes the bass by subtracting the offbeats, and names the nearest note with the cents offset.
